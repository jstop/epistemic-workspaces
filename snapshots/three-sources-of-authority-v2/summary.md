# People rely on another party's word on three overlapping grounds: office (formally constituted, with holders who can be sanctioned), community (the people relying chose to, and can leave), and open challenge (a claim stands because anyone could contest it and it held; a proven track record is a form of this, with experience doing the contesting). The grounds blend in practice but differ in how they fail: an office can be captured, a community's word does not carry to distant strangers, and open challenge works only when participants are real, accountable and free to dissent. Each failure is partly mitigated by the other two, though a powerful enough actor could degrade all three at once. For the specific problem of knowing whose word to rely on across borders, no new global authority is needed: the three grounds working together are enough, resting on existing national courts for enforcement. This arrangement does not address compliance problems, where parties accept the facts and still break agreements because it pays; those require consequences that being believed cannot supply.

## Known Objections

### Unresolved
- [undercutting] Survivorship and selection: the cross-border trade that visibly functions on reputation plus national courts is the trade that was feasible under those conditions. Exchanges that never happened because no party's word could be relied on (e.g. with counterparties in jurisdictions whose courts are not trusted or whose judgments are not recognised) do not appear in the record. The observed success therefore does not show the arrangement is 'enough'; it may show only that it is enough for the subset of cases it already serves, leaving a large unserved domain where a supranational mechanism (or something else) might add real value. *(on: Cross-border reliance already functions on the three grounds plus national-court enforcement; the best explanation is that this combination suffices, so a new global authority adds little)*
- [rebutting] The three grounds are not independent in the way the mitigation argument needs. Offices are staffed from communities; open-challenge forums are owned by platforms that are themselves offices or community-like clubs; communities form their judgments by reading the output of offices and forums. Correlated failure is therefore the normal case, not a rare exception reserved for a 'powerful enough actor': a dominant state or platform routinely captures the regulator, hosts and shapes the forum, and defines the community's exit options at once. If failures are strongly correlated, 'each is partly mitigated by the other two' overstates the resilience, and the thesis's conclusion that no additional mechanism is needed loses its main support. *(on: Because the grounds fail in different ways, the failure of one leaves the others available to expose or substitute for it)*
