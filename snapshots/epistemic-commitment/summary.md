# Epistemic resolution — the capacity for public discourse to converge on justified conclusions — is substantially facilitated by participants whose identities persist across encounters, because persistent identity provides a primary (though not sole) mechanism by which arguments accumulate across debates, defeaters are tracked, and dialectical progress is preserved. In the absence of robust institutional memory or equivalent structural substitutes, and assuming a baseline of good-faith participation, the loss of persistent identity severely degrades discourse toward repetitive, zero-state exchanges.

*Pattern: abduction | Confidence: 72%*

- **Claim:** persistent-identity enables argument-accumulation-across-debates (85%)
  - When participants have persistent identities, their prior arguments, concessions, and commitments carry forward into new encounters. Interlocutors can reference what someone previously claimed, creating a ratchet effect where dialectical ground need not be re-traversed.
- **Claim:** persistent-identity enables defeater-tracking-across-encounters (80%)
  - Defeaters (objections, counterexamples, undercutting evidence) raised against a participant's position can be tracked and attributed. This prevents the repeated assertion of already-defeated arguments and forces dialectical evolution rather than repetition.
- **Claim:** loss-of-persistent-identity causes degradation-to-zero-state-discourse (70%)
  - Without persistent identity, each encounter resets to a zero-state where no prior commitments bind participants. This produces repetitive exchanges because previously refuted positions can be reasserted without cost, and convergence becomes structurally impeded.
- **Claim:** persistent-identity is-primary-but-not-sole-mechanism-for dialectical-progress-preservation (65%)
  - The thesis hedges that persistent identity is a 'primary though not sole' mechanism. Other mechanisms (institutional memory, written records, moderation structures) can partially substitute, but identity remains the most common and accessible vehicle for preserving dialectical progress in ordinary discourse.
- **Claim:** absence-of-institutional-memory is-scope-condition-for identity-dependence-of-epistemic-resolution (75%)
  - The thesis is explicitly conditioned on the absence of robust institutional memory or structural substitutes. Where such substitutes exist (e.g. peer review systems, parliamentary records, wiki-style collaborative knowledge bases), the dependence on personal identity is reduced. The claim is strongest precisely where such infrastructure is weakest.

- **Objection [ACTIVE]:** The structural-substitute undercount problem: The thesis concedes that institutional memory can substitute for persistent identity, but may underestimate how prevalent and effective such substitutes already are. Wikipedia, legal precedent systems, academic peer review, and AI-assisted summarization tools all provide identity-independent mechanisms for accumulating arguments and tracking defeaters. If these structural substitutes are more widespread than the thesis assumes, the claimed dependence on persistent identity is substantially weakened — not because the mechanism is wrong, but because the scope condition ('in the absence of robust institutional memory') rarely obtains in the discourse contexts that matter most.
