# Identity is a constitutive precondition of accountable discourse. The practice of binding utterances to addressable, persistent agents — agents who can be called by name and expected to answer for what they said — is what makes normative speech possible. Without such agents, there is no commitment, only acoustic or textual events that resemble commitment. This is a structural claim about what discourse is, not a claim about how thick the identity must be or how it should be implemented.

- **Claim:** normative-speech requires binding-utterances-to-persistent-agents (85%)
  - For speech to carry normative force (promises, assertions, commitments), utterances must be attributable to agents who persist across time and can be held to account. This is the core mechanism the thesis identifies.
- **Claim:** commitment is-impossible-without addressable-agents (80%)
  - Without agents who can be called by name and expected to answer, utterances that look like commitments are merely acoustic or textual events that resemble commitment. Commitment requires someone to be committed — a who that persists.
- **Claim:** discourse-without-identity collapses-into mere-events (77%)
  - The counterfactual test: if you remove persistent, addressable agents from discourse, what remains is not defective discourse but something categorically different — acoustic or textual events that merely resemble commitment. The normative dimension vanishes entirely.
- **Claim:** identity-requirement is structural-not-substantive (75%)
  - The thesis claims to be about the structure of discourse itself — what discourse constitutively is — rather than prescribing how thick or thin identity must be, or how it should be technically or socially implemented. This is a transcendental-style argument about conditions of possibility.
- **Claim:** addressability-and-persistence are-minimal-conditions-of agentive-identity-in-discourse (78%)
  - The thesis identifies two minimal features of the required identity: (1) addressability — the agent can be called by name or reference, and (2) persistence — the agent endures across time such that past utterances remain bound to it. These are the structural minima for accountability.

- **Objection [ACTIVE]:** The Foucauldian/post-structuralist challenge: discourse may be constituted not by individual agents but by subject-positions within power structures. On this view, 'identity' is an effect of discourse rather than its precondition — the arrow of constitution runs the other way. Speakers do not pre-exist the discursive formations that produce them. If so, the thesis commits a foundationalist error by treating identity as prior to the practice it allegedly enables. The normative force of speech may derive from institutional and structural positions rather than from persistent individual agents.
