Coordination failures arise from a mixture of structural conflicts (zero-sum incentive landscapes, power asymmetries) and trust or identification deficits, with the relative weight varying substantially across domains — and infrastructure interventions that address trust components can, as Ostrom and others have shown, sometimes shift effective payoff structures even where underlying interests partly conflict. Where coordination failures do stem significantly from trust and identification deficits, shared infrastructure — including identity systems enabling counterparty verification and reputational stakes — may create possibility spaces for non-zero-sum dynamics, though this mechanism remains underspecified and its scalability beyond domain-specific applications (such as escrow, credit registries, and platform reputation systems) to a globally integrated identity layer is empirically undemonstrated. A globally accessible identity system emphasizing accountable anonymity could, in principle, create structural preconditions for broader coordination, but this proposal faces three serious challenges whose severity remains uncertain: first, that capture resistance may degrade as the system's value increases, and a captured global identity system could prove worse than the coordination failures it aimed to address; second, that the separability of identity verification from surveillance and control at scale remains an undemonstrated design aspiration rather than an established principle, though partial approximations may prove workable; third, that cultural and institutional differences may impede the interoperability and voluntary adoption such a system requires, particularly where network effects risk rendering nominally voluntary participation functionally coercive. These challenges, combined with the asymmetric risk profile in the global-system case specifically, suggest that proponents of a unified capture-resistant global identity infrastructure bear a substantial — though not uniquely unprecedented — burden to demonstrate feasibility, separability from surveillance, and a net-positive risk profile through rigorous incremental validation (including regional and domain-specific deployments) before any integration at civilizational scale, while acknowledging that some degree of learning-through-deployment is unavoidable for complex sociotechnical systems and that the costs of unresolved coordination failures also warrant serious weight in the overall risk calculus.
