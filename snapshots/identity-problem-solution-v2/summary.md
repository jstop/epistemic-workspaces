# PROBLEM: A car-share fleet in Lisbon receives a booking from a stranger whose only standing comes from an ecovillage the fleet has never heard of. Within minutes the fleet must decide whether to hand over a car, and it needs two answers: is this one real person (not a bot or synthetic identity), and if the booking turns out to be fraudulent, is there a party it can hold accountable? Today it has two options. (1) Accept only a large certifier everyone recognises: one point that can be breached, pressured, or refuse people with no appeal. (2) Accept only people its own network vouches for: this stranger is turned away, and weakly checked lists let fakes in cheaply. SOLUTION: Split three jobs that both options bundle. (a) Certifying: several independent certifiers each check a person, each is legally liable for its own defective checks (a certified identity that proves synthetic, duplicated, or not the person who presented) and insured for that liability. Certifiers keep no shared store of personal data; they may share only a minimal, non-identifying signal — a salted hash of a credential or a document-level revocation flag — sufficient to detect a person re-presenting the same identity after a fraud-based refusal, and that signal carries no attributes, no refusal reason, and no record of who queried it. (b) Grading: one public scale, run by a body that does not itself certify, states how thorough each check was and what inputs it relied on. (c) Accepting: the fleet publishes which certifiers and minimum grade it accepts; the ecovillage keeps its own, different list. SCOPE: The architecture answers the identity question and the accountability question for identity fraud. It does not make a certifier a guarantor of the customer's later conduct; a correctly verified real person who steals the car remains the fleet's problem, to be covered by its existing insurance and deposit and by the fact that a real, accountable individual can now be named. Any certifier that also offers conduct guarantees is selling a separate, priced product on top of certification, not part of this design. SUCCESS TESTS: (i) if one certifier refuses the person for a non-fraud reason (capacity, geography, documents it cannot read), another certifier the fleet accepts can still certify them; a refusal grounded in detected identity fraud is propagated via the minimal signal in (a), so this is a right to a second check, not a second identity; (ii) the fleet can accept the stranger at its minimum grade without knowing the ecovillage; (iii) if the certified identity proves fake or stolen, the fleet can name a liable, insured certifier that can actually pay; if the identity was genuine, the fleet can name the real individual; (iv) no party holds attribute-level personal data on people it did not check itself; the only cross-certifier state is the non-identifying signal in (a), and test (iv) is failed if that signal can be reversed to attributes or used to profile who queried it. BUILDABILITY TEST: (v) each of the three jobs names who runs it, who pays for it, and a first real user with a reason to adopt it; any fix that requires a party to act against its own interest, such as a relying party turning away paying customers, fails this test. This test is currently unmet for two load-bearing assumptions — that enough certifiers will enter the fleet's accepted list, and that insurers will underwrite per-check liability at a price certifiers can pass on — and the thesis stands only as a design until both are shown by at least one live instance. PRIOR-ART TEST: (vi) eIDAS trusted lists and levels of assurance, NIST SP 800-63 assurance levels, and Osmio's certification already supply plural providers, assurance tiers, relying-party choice of provider and level, and (for QTSPs) insured liability without a shared database. The thesis therefore does not claim a new trust architecture. What it claims to add is narrower: a single cross-framework grading scale that exposes the inputs behind each check so a small relying party can compare certifiers it has never dealt with; private, per-relying-party acceptance lists that are published as machine-readable policy rather than negotiated bilaterally; and the minimal fraud-signal in (a) that resolves the certifier-shopping gap those frameworks leave open. If, on inspection, each of these is already a deployment option under eIDAS 2.0 or an existing scheme, the correct description is a deployment profile of existing systems, and the thesis should be restated as such.

### Unresolved
- [rebutting] eIDAS 2.0 may already supply each claimed addition as a deployment option: the EU Digital Identity Wallet framework includes relying-party registration with declared intended-use and accepted-attestation policies (a machine-readable per-relying-party acceptance list), conformity-assessed levels of assurance that reference the proofing inputs used (an input-exposing grade, at least within the EU), and wallet-level credential revocation and status lists (a non-identifying revocation signal). If these cover the Lisbon fleet's case, the correct description — by the thesis's own test (vi) — is a deployment profile of eIDAS 2.0, and the root claim should be restated as such rather than as a distinct architecture. *(on: eIDAS and NIST tier and list providers within their own framework, but neither publishes a cross-framework input-exposing grade, machine-readable per-relying-party acceptance policy, or a cross-certifier fraud-refusal signal — so the additions appear genuinely absent)*
- [undermining] The analogy to hashed-lookup systems breaks on entropy and on the threat model. National ID and passport numbers have small, structured keyspaces, so a salted hash of a document number is dictionary-attackable by any party holding the salt — which every participating certifier must hold to query. A certifier that already has attributes on a person it checked can also test hashes for people it did not check, converting the "non-identifying" signal into a cross-certifier membership oracle. And unless the lookup is run as a private-set-membership or oblivious protocol, the operator of the signal sees who queried and when, which is exactly the profiling test (iv) forbids. The signal may therefore fail test (iv) in practice even though revocation bits succeed in the certificate world, where serials are high-entropy and public. *(on: Hashed lookups and attribute-free revocation bits are deployed elsewhere; the same mechanism can flag re-presentation after a fraud refusal without a shared personal-data store)*
